The status quo

Too many warnings, too early to be sure.

A conjunction analyst reads Conjunction Data Messages (CDMs), one per update, and decides whether the satellite should burn to move out of the way. The burn has to be planned about two days before closest approach, before the most accurate messages arrive. Today that means checking each warning against fixed thresholds by hand.

Fifteen warnings, hovering near 1 km.

From 6.7 days out to the decision point, the miss distance wanders between 878 m and 1,531 m. Pc stays near 10−10: by probability alone it is harmless.

What the rules say

Elevated. Put it on a list.

The latest message says 1,089 m and Pc 5×10−11. That is inside 5 km but outside 1 km, so a threshold filter tiers it Elevated and moves on.

What the model says

One in three ends Critical.

Trained on how thousands of past encounters played out, the model gives P(Critical) 33%, P(Elevated) 64%, P(Safe) 2.5%, with a miss forecast of 1,088 m. These probabilities are calibrated: events scored like this ended Critical about a third of the time.

The result

Flagged Critical, with two days to spare.

33% is above the 15% Critical threshold, so the event goes to the top of the queue with its reason and drivers (miss distance small) while there is still time to plan a burn.

And if it did crash?

The tiers say how likely a collision is. The cascade says how bad it would be. A crash makes a cloud of fragments, and those fragments can hit other satellites in the same orbit: the first link of a Kessler chain reaction. Apsis estimates this for every encounter.

1 · MassFrom each object's size and drag. Capped at 10 t, because big solar panels made some satellites look 96 t heavy.
2 · EnergyHow hard the hit is, per gram of the bigger object. At 40 J/g or more both objects shatter (NASA Standard Breakup Model).
3 · FragmentsHow many pieces of 10 cm or more, the size radars can track.
4 · Chain riskFragments spread over a ±50 km shell; the yearly chance they hit another mission there (Kessler particle-in-a-box).
Two Criticals can look the same on probability. The one that would make thousands of fragments goes first. Event 825 shows why both matter: Safe by probability, yet it carries the worst chain risk if it ever hit.

Where it fails, and how you would know

Some danger arrives after the decision.

All 3 Criticals we called Safe on the holdout became Critical by geometry alone: the miss distance closed below 1,000 m after the 2-day decision point while Pc stayed under 10−4. Event 1936 went from 13,748 m to 657 m. No tool working at T−2 days could have seen it.

Each missed event is listed on screen with its cause. Raising every forecast miss under 1,500 m would catch 12 more out of fold for 92 more alerts, so that dial is left to the analyst.

Our risk forecaster lost

Predicting the final Pc did worse than "final = latest" (ESA loss 9.47 vs 4.35). Most events collapse to zero risk while the dangerous ones stay flat. Only 12 of 97 teams beat this baseline in ESA's own competition.

→ Measured, and the pipeline falls back to the baseline automatically.

More false alarms

Critical precision drops from 0.81 to 0.68 on the holdout (0.86 to 0.69 on the hidden test set). On a balanced score (F2) it is roughly a tie: 0.858 vs 0.862.

→ Shown in the Evaluation tab. The case rests on a missed collision costing more than a review.

An idea that failed

We expected low Pc from poor tracking to hide danger. On real outcomes none of 279 flagged Safe events ended Critical.

→ Kept as a tracking-quality note, never a warning.

Thin evidence

One CDM, a stale CDM over 5 days out, or missing covariance: the rule tier is wrong almost twice as often.

→ The tool refuses to forecast. 91 test events go to a person.
